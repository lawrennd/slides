How Information Flows in Organisations

In a classical corporate hierarchy, information travels vertically. Strategic decisions flow downward from the CEO through the C-suite (CFO, CIO, COO) to functional departments. Data and reports flow upward. External signals from customers and markets enter at the top. Each layer introduces delay and compression.

Hub-and-Spoke

In a hub-and-spoke model, all information routes through a central node. This can result in low latency (good command and control) but the central hub can become overloaded.

Peer-to-Peer

In a peer-to-peer network, teams communicate directly with each other through adjacent and cross-cutting channels, without routing through a central authority. This structure is resilient and high-bandwidth, but requires shared protocols and trust. It mirrors how open-source software communities and federated data ecosystems operate.

The API Mandate

  • All teams will henceforth expose their data and functionality through service interfaces.
  • Teams must communicate with each other through these interfaces.

  • There will be no other form of inter-process communication allowed: no direct linking, no direct reads of another team’s data store, no shared-memory model, no back-doors whatsoever. The only communication allowed is via service interface calls over the network.

  • It doesn’t matter what technology they use.
  • All service interfaces, without exception, must be designed from the ground up to be externalizable. That is to say, the team must plan and design to be able to expose the interface to developers in the outside world. No exceptions.

Agentic Debt

  • Agentic AI could pay down technical and intellectual debt.
  • But it can create agentic debt: delegation without authority/authorship
  • Delegation of workflows without crisp boundaries.
  • Agentic debt is about unsafe or illegible delegation.

Judgment Layer: Organisations as emulsions

  • Organisations are a stable mixture: automatable routines and irreducible human context.
  • In a working emulsion the two phases are inseparable — like oil and water held in suspension.
  • The judgment layer is the interface: what gets challenged, what gets waived, what triggers a halt.

What the judgment layer looks like

  • Approval exceptions: “we usually accept this class of risk without escalation.”
  • Escalation triggers: “this pattern always goes to a human, even if the rule says approve.”
  • Context-dependent waivers: policy says X, but the situation means Y is safer.

Close: What Diligence Should Underwrite

Strategic Priorities for Investors

  • People first, not model first
  • Ask whether the judgment layer is explicit
  • Underwrite authority where consequence lives
  • Watch for intellectual and agentic debt

AI arrives workflow by workflow

Letter in the FT: AI arrives workflow by workflow — markets err when they value software groups as if AI were one centralised deployment.

A mistake markets make

  • Markets often treat AI as one centralised deployment
  • Value arrives workflow by workflow, with people who know the work
  • “Centralised deployment” is a supplier story; useful deployment is a demand story

Implication for software groups

  • Do not value a portfolio as if AI were a single centre-led programme
  • Underwrite the local judgment layer in each workflow
  • Support demand-side adoption — do not only sell a supplier story

Takeaways

  • AI commoditises many artefacts — not the judgment layer
  • Advantage sits in attention, trust, and legible human authority
  • Intellectual and agentic debt are balance-sheet risks
  • Diligence should underwrite judgment as carefully as the model

APPENDIX

The Good Regulator Theorem

  • Every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system.
  • The judgement layer is the regulator: it must still model what it oversees.
  • If the system outruns the model, regulation fails — even if the tools still run.

Why we minimise models

  • We minimise so that we can maintain a model.
  • Smallest decision-relevant representation that still supports oversight.
  • Prefer decomposable systems over opaque monoliths.

Against maximisation by default

  • “Use the largest model” is a default, not a design.
  • Capability without a modellable envelope is how debt compounds.
  • Ask: what is the least system that still earns regulated trust?

Emulate, Do Not Replicate

Anything we can measure, a machine can probably emulate. That is not the same as replicating us.

  • Our intelligence is shaped by its context
  • Measure a capability, fit parameters, recreate the score
  • You cannot simply replicate us, because the context is specific
The cover is not the argument. One accidental equation in the book (\(E=mc^2\)) because everyone knows it. Do not define intelligence. Ask whether AGI-as-better-on-all-scores is even the right question.

The Cut

If every machine capability slices something away from us, does the slicing go forever?

  • Greek atomists: you stop — the world is made of what you cannot cut
  • Computer scientists often say no: humans disappear
  • Other answers: empathy, suffering, God
Democritus, not a physics lecture. Accelerators splitting the atom just means we misnamed the atom: quarks sit below. In a public-understanding talk, pause here.

What the Machine Cannot Have

The machine cannot have our weaknesses and our vulnerabilities. They are intrinsic to us.

  • Anything I can score, I can emulate
  • Vulnerabilities and limitations are fundamental
  • Key among them: communicate versus compute

  • AGI without that limitation is not general. It is specific to us
  • You can emulate it, but it is not the same
